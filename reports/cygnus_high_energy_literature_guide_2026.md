# The Cygnus Cocoon and Bubble in gamma rays and cosmic rays

**A literature-guided introduction, current through 27 July 2026**

## Executive answer

The “Cygnus thing” discussed in high-energy astrophysics is not a single sharply bounded object. It is extended gamma-ray emission seen toward the Cygnus X star-forming complex. The GeV-scale feature discovered with Fermi-LAT is usually called the **Cygnus Cocoon**; the much larger emission measured up to petaelectronvolt (PeV) energies by LHAASO is called the **Cygnus Bubble**. Both overlap on the sky with the massive stellar association Cygnus OB2, but they also overlap with molecular clouds, pulsars and pulsar-wind nebulae, the gamma-Cygni supernova remnant, and the more distant microquasar Cygnus X-3. They should not automatically be treated as one physical emitter [1, 5, 7, 8, 12].

What is secure is that the region contains freshly accelerated non-thermal particles and emits gamma rays from GeV energies to the PeV domain [2-4, 7]. What is not secure is whether one accelerator and one particle population produce the entire spectrum. The current evidence instead permits, and increasingly favours, several components: stellar-wind-accelerated electrons may dominate part of the GeV-to-TeV emission; protons from a past supernova or another transient may dominate tens to hundreds of TeV; Galactic background cosmic rays contribute; and Cygnus X-3 is now independently established as a variable PeV gamma-ray source that can contribute at the highest energies [5, 8, 12, 16].

The scientific importance is that Cygnus is a nearby laboratory for three connected problems: whether massive-star environments are major Galactic cosmic-ray factories, how particles escape and diffuse around their sources, and what accelerates Galactic particles to and beyond the cosmic-ray knee. The gamma rays prove that extreme particles are present, but gamma rays alone do not yet uniquely identify the accelerator or even the hadronic versus leptonic channel at all energies [2, 5, 8, 11].

## 1. The names: five related but different things

| Name | Meaning in this literature | What not to assume |
|---|---|---|
| **Cygnus** | A direction in the Galactic plane containing many superposed sources and clouds. | It is not one object at one distance. |
| **Cygnus X** | A roughly 10-degree, massive star-forming complex recognised historically through extended radio emission; modern work places its important star-forming material in several distance layers, with Cygnus OB2 near 1.6-1.7 kpc [1, 13]. | The “X” means extended radio emission, not an X-ray source, and not Cygnus X-1 or X-3. |
| **Cygnus OB2** | The dominant massive-star association near the centre of Cygnus X. It is young, rich in O and Wolf-Rayet stars, and mechanically powerful, but spatially loose and internally structured [8, 14]. | It is not a compact, coeval star cluster, and a single coherent cluster wind cannot simply be assumed. |
| **Cygnus Cocoon** | The hard, extended GeV excess found by Fermi-LAT and interpreted as freshly accelerated particles filling cavities made by massive-star feedback. Later Fermi analysis decomposed it into multiple components [2, 5]. | It is not directly a material shell, nor is its original 2011 template the final morphology. |
| **Cygnus Bubble** | LHAASO’s at-least-100-square-degree, diffuse ultra-high-energy gamma-ray structure extending to a few PeV [7]. | It is an observational gamma-ray structure; it is not automatically identical to the Fermi Cocoon or to the older soft-X-ray “Cygnus Superbubble.” |

The terminology is historically layered. The **physical superbubble** means a hot, low-density cavity inflated by stellar winds and past supernovae. The **Fermi Cocoon** means a gamma-ray excess/population of young particles. The **LHAASO Bubble** means a larger UHE gamma-ray morphology. Their relationship is one of the questions being investigated, not a definition [1, 5, 7, 8].

## 2. What gamma rays tell us

Charged cosmic rays are deflected by Galactic magnetic fields, so arrival directions at Earth generally do not point back cleanly to their sources. Gamma rays travel nearly straight and are therefore used as tracers of where energetic particles interact. In a **hadronic** interpretation, accelerated protons or nuclei collide with gas, produce neutral pions, and the pions decay to gamma rays; charged pions from the same interaction also produce neutrinos. In a **leptonic** interpretation, high-energy electrons upscatter ambient photons by inverse Compton scattering and can also radiate synchrotron and bremsstrahlung emission. Gas maps, radiation fields, radio/X-ray limits, spectra, and energy-dependent morphology are therefore needed to distinguish the channels [2, 6, 8, 11].

PeV gamma rays are especially important. If they are produced hadronically, the parent proton energies are typically an order of magnitude higher, so the LHAASO Bubble requires acceleration into the multi-PeV or approximately 10-PeV regime. That is why LHAASO described the region as containing a “super-PeVatron.” The gamma-ray detection demonstrates an extreme accelerator in this direction, but it does not by itself show that Cygnus OB2 is that accelerator [7, 8, 12].

## 3. High-energy history: the milestones that changed the picture

### Before the Cocoon

Cygnus X was identified as diffuse radio emission in 1952. Subsequent radio work separated compact H II regions from the broader thermal complex, while soft-X-ray observations revealed a very large shell-like structure historically called the Cygnus Superbubble. The reality of a single coherent X-ray superbubble remained uncertain because multiple foreground and background structures overlap along this tangent view of the Local Arm [8, 13].

Gamma-ray observations before Fermi already showed that the direction was complicated. EGRET detected extended GeV sources; HEGRA discovered TeV J2032+4130 near Cygnus OB2; Milagro later measured a much brighter and more extended TeV excess. Some compact emission is now associated with known objects such as the pulsar/PWN around PSR J2032+4127 and gamma-Cygni, but those objects do not account for all of the diffuse emission [3, 6, 8].

### 2011: Fermi-LAT discovers the Cocoon

The defining discovery paper used 1-100 GeV Fermi-LAT images to identify a roughly 50-pc-wide hard gamma-ray excess filling cavities carved by young stellar feedback. The key inference was not merely “there are gamma rays,” but that the particles were younger and harder than the ordinary Galactic cosmic-ray population. This made the Cocoon the prototype observational case for cosmic-ray acceleration and early propagation inside a massive-star superbubble [2].

### 2014: ARGO-YBJ connects the source to TeV energies

ARGO-YBJ found a roughly 1.8-degree extended source from about 0.2 to 10 TeV whose size and spectrum connected smoothly to the Fermi Cocoon after accounting for overlapping sources. This established that the phenomenon did not end at GeV energies and strengthened a hadronic interpretation, although the angular resolution did not allow a decisive morphological decomposition [3].

### 2021: HAWC resolves the 1-100 TeV Cocoon

HAWC measured extended 1-100 TeV emission and argued that it was produced by freshly accelerated particles with energies of roughly 10-1000 TeV. The spectrum and radial behaviour were consistent with continuous particle injection, and a hadronic model was favoured under the environmental assumptions used in that analysis. The result made massive-star environments serious competitors or complements to isolated supernova remnants as Galactic cosmic-ray accelerators [4].

### 2023: Fermi shows that the “Cocoon” is not one component

Using about 13 years of LAT data and improved interstellar-emission modelling, Astiasarain et al. found a broad component with \(r_{68}=4.4^\circ\), a spectrally distinct central component tracing ionised gas, and a likely additional related component. A single diffusion-loss population can reproduce the main structures in several very different parameter regimes, but both hadronic and leptonic solutions remain possible. The important lesson is that the original one-template Cocoon is an oversimplification [5].

### 2024: LHAASO discovers the UHE Bubble

LHAASO reported diffuse emission spanning at least 100 square degrees and extending from TeV energies to a few PeV. It found a quasi-spherical diffuse component, central brightening, and hotspots associated with massive molecular material. The morphology and spectrum could be reproduced phenomenologically by continuous proton injection from a central accelerator, leading to the “super-PeVatron” interpretation. The paper established the UHE phenomenon but did not uniquely identify the source [7].

### 2024-2025: the collective-wind interpretation is challenged

Menchiari et al. showed that a hadronic model based on acceleration at a collective Cygnus OB2 wind-termination shock can fit much of the gamma-ray spectrum if diffusion is close to Bohm or Kraichnan-like; Kolmogorov transport fails energetically, and the Fermi central morphology remains difficult [6]. However, a three-dimensional hydrodynamic simulation tailored to the observed stellar distribution found that Cygnus OB2 is too loose to form the assumed large-scale collective termination shock. It makes a superbubble, but its individual stellar winds do not merge into the simple compact-cluster flow required by that model [8, 14].

Härer et al. then constructed a multi-component model: inverse-Compton emission from electrons accelerated at individual stellar-wind termination shocks accounts for the emission below about 10 TeV, while protons from a powerful, approximately 50-kyr-old undetected supernova remnant in the low-density superbubble account for roughly 10 TeV to PeV emission. Their three-dimensional gas model reproduces important aspects of the LHAASO morphology. This is currently the most useful integrated review/model, but it is a proposed explanation rather than a unique solution [8].

### July 2026: Cygnus X-3 changes the highest-energy story

LHAASO has now detected Cygnus X-3 itself as a variable gamma-ray source from about 0.06 to 3.7 PeV at approximately 10-sigma significance. Month-scale variability and evidence for orbital modulation locate at least this UHE component in or close to the binary, rather than in the 1.6-kpc Cygnus OB2 association. A hadronic jet interpretation requires proton acceleration to tens of PeV. This confirms one real super-PeVatron in the same line of sight [12].

A long-term MAGIC/Fermi-LAT study did not detect Cygnus X-3 at very-high energies in the MAGIC data, but its upper limits remain compatible with the high-energy measurements; source variability and the different energy ranges must therefore be kept in the comparison [18].

The LHAASO result does **not** yet prove that Cygnus X-3 powers the entire several-degree LHAASO Bubble. A July 2026 preprint argues that protons escaping from Cygnus X-3 form the observed large halo, while another 2026 transport study finds that a steady, non-relativistic Cygnus OB2 source requires extreme diffusion assumptions and explicitly raises background sources as an alternative. These are active interpretations, not settled identifications [15, 16].

## 4. The physical picture

### The environment

Cygnus OB2 contains many massive stars whose winds inject a mechanical luminosity of order a few \(10^{38}\) erg s\(^{-1}\). Over several Myr, those winds heat and evacuate gas, producing a low-density interior surrounded by denser atomic and molecular structures. The association has a half-mass scale of about 15 pc, an age spread of roughly 3-7 Myr, and line-of-sight substructure; modern Gaia-based work places its main population near 1.6-1.7 kpc [8, 14].

That geometry creates the central modelling tension. Strong shocks and long particle confinement are easier in a low-density hot cavity, but hadronic gamma-ray production is brighter where target gas is dense. A model must therefore place the accelerator, the trapped particles, and the atomic/molecular targets consistently in three dimensions. Foreground clouds can look correlated in a two-dimensional image without being physically close to the accelerator [5, 8].

### Candidate accelerators

1. **Individual O/WR stellar-wind shocks.** They supply power continuously for Myr and can plausibly accelerate electrons and ions to tens of TeV. In the Härer et al. model, inverse-Compton emission from their electrons explains the GeV-to-low-TeV component with an electron efficiency near one per cent. They are not expected to explain the PeV photons under standard non-relativistic-shock conditions [8].

2. **A collective Cygnus OB2 wind-termination shock.** This is attractive because a large shock operating for Myr can continuously inject cosmic rays. Spectral fits can work for strongly suppressed, Bohm/Kraichnan-like diffusion, but the observed loose stellar distribution does not generate the required coherent shock in current hydrodynamic simulations [6, 14].

3. **A past supernova inside the superbubble.** Cygnus OB2 is old and massive enough that past core-collapse supernovae are plausible, while a remnant expanding in hot tenuous gas could become very large and faint. A powerful event about 50 kyr ago can reproduce the LHAASO spectrum and energy-dependent morphology in one recent model, provided diffusion is strongly suppressed and clouds lie near the bubble edge [8].

4. **Known gamma-Cygni SNR and pulsar/PWN sources.** They contribute to the crowded field, but their present spectra and locations do not naturally account for the entire LHAASO diffuse component. Escape from gamma-Cygni at an earlier stage remains a possible contributor requiring asymmetric transport modelling [8].

5. **Cygnus X-3.** The 2026 LHAASO detection proves that this microquasar produces variable PeV gamma rays and accelerates hadrons to tens of PeV in the favoured interpretation. It certainly contaminates or contributes to the highest-energy sky map; whether its escaped particles make the whole diffuse Bubble remains under debate [12, 15].

6. **The Galactic cosmic-ray sea and unresolved sources.** Ordinary Galactic cosmic rays interacting with the unusually large gas column contribute diffuse emission, and uncertainties in this foreground/background are important above hundreds of TeV. Unresolved sources can also be blended into wide-field air-shower measurements [8, 16].

## 5. What is established, inferred, and still open

### Established observationally

- There is hard, extended GeV emission beyond standard interstellar-emission models, and its modern morphology has multiple components [2, 5].
- Extended TeV emission overlaps the Cocoon and persists to approximately 100 TeV [3, 4].
- LHAASO sees a much larger UHE structure extending to a few PeV; therefore particles of at least PeV energies exist in this direction [7].
- Cygnus X-3 is independently a variable 0.06-3.7 PeV gamma-ray source [12].
- A published IceCube-data search found no statistically significant neutrino emission from the whole LHAASO Bubble and set upper limits. Thus, a hadronic origin for the extended emission is not yet confirmed by a source neutrino counterpart [11].

### Strong but model-dependent inferences

- Particle transport around the source is slower than average Galactic diffusion, otherwise a hard and centrally concentrated population would disperse too quickly. The required suppression and its spatial dependence vary substantially among models [4-6, 16].
- More than one particle population or accelerator is likely because the spectrum, morphology, target fields, and maximum energies change across GeV, TeV, and PeV bands [5, 8, 12].
- Stellar feedback created a low-density cavity around Cygnus OB2, but the exact three-dimensional boundary and its relationship to the radio, X-ray, GeV, and UHE structures are not established [8, 14].

### Open questions

- Is the 10-1000 TeV diffuse component mainly hadronic, leptonic, or an energy-dependent mixture? [5, 8, 16]
- Did a now-faint supernova remnant inject the UHE protons, or is the dominant source Cygnus X-3, gamma-Cygni, another transient, or several sources? [8, 12, 15]
- How much of the LHAASO morphology follows physical gas near Cygnus OB2 rather than foreground/background material? [8, 16]
- What is the diffusion tensor and its energy dependence inside and outside the cavity? [4-6, 16]
- Does the Cygnus region make a measurable contribution to the cosmic-ray spectrum at Earth or to the knee? Existing answers range from a minimal local contribution to models in which Cygnus is important; these remain propagation-dependent hypotheses [10, 17].

## 6. Why this matters for the Galactic cosmic-ray problem

The standard supernova-remnant paradigm has enough total power to maintain Galactic cosmic rays, but routinely accelerating protons to the knee is difficult for ordinary remnants. Massive-star environments supply complementary ingredients: long-lived winds, repeated supernovae, enriched Wolf-Rayet material, large cavities, turbulence, and multiple shocks. Population models show that supernovae inside a subset of massive stellar clusters could contribute from the TeV band to beyond the knee, but those results depend on rare fast shocks and strong turbulence that have not been demonstrated observationally in every system [10].

Cygnus is therefore valuable less as a guaranteed single-source explanation and more as a resolved laboratory. It lets us compare stellar demographics and mechanical power, three-dimensional gas, gamma-ray spectra, gamma-ray morphology, and neutrino limits in one nearby direction. If the particle injection and diffusion history can be disentangled here, it tests whether massive-star complexes are merely bright local curiosities or an important Galactic source class [5, 8, 10, 11].

## 7. Recommended reading order

### If you read only four papers

1. **Härer et al. (2025), “Deciphering the gamma-ray emission in the Cygnus region” [8].** Start here. It is a 20-page modern synthesis of the objects, gas, distances, competing accelerators, and broadband data. Its preferred lepto-hadronic model is not final, especially after the 2026 Cygnus X-3 result, but it is the clearest map of the problem.

2. **Astiasarain et al. (2023), “Multiple emission components in the Cygnus cocoon detected from Fermi-LAT observations” [5].** Read this to understand what the GeV Cocoon actually looks like in modern data and why a single-zone picture is inadequate.

3. **LHAASO Collaboration (2024), “An ultrahigh-energy gamma-ray bubble powered by a super PeVatron” [7].** This is the observational UHE breakthrough and the source of the “Cygnus Bubble/super-PeVatron” language.

4. **LHAASO Collaboration (2026), “Cygnus X-3: A variable petaelectronvolt gamma-ray source” [12].** This is essential current context: it proves that a background microquasar in the same direction contributes at PeV energies.

### Then add these

5. **Ackermann et al. (2011) [2]** for the original Fermi discovery and the meaning of “freshly accelerated.”
6. **HAWC Collaboration (2021) [4]** for the TeV spectrum, morphology, and continuous-injection argument.
7. **Vieu et al. (2024) [14]** for the hydrodynamic reason a simple collective Cygnus OB2 wind shock is doubtful.
8. **Menchiari et al. (2024) [6]** as a careful example of the collective-wind hadronic hypothesis and its diffusion requirements.
9. **Li, Blasi & Amato (2026) [16]** for the newest transport critique; it was accepted by A&A but only the preprint was available at the cut-off date.

## 8. Bottom line

The safest present statement is:

> Cygnus is a multi-source, multi-distance high-energy laboratory. The Cocoon/Bubble observations establish local fresh-particle acceleration and PeV gamma-ray production, but they do not establish one accelerator. Cygnus OB2, past supernovae, molecular targets, Galactic cosmic rays, and the now-confirmed PeV microquasar Cygnus X-3 all contribute to the interpretation. A multi-component model is currently more credible than “one cluster, one shock, one spectrum.”

The most decisive future discriminants are energy-resolved morphology with LHAASO and CTAO, better three-dimensional gas distances, deep radio/X-ray searches for a relic remnant, and more sensitive neutrino observations. Each probes a different degeneracy: transport, target placement, hidden accelerators, and hadronic content, respectively [8, 11, 16].
